# Serbian/Bosnian Tech-House/Pop Lyric Style Guide

A compact field guide for writing club-ready Serbian/Bosnian pop lyrics based on the 10-song research corpus.

---

## 1. Structural Recipe

- **Total lines**: 30–50.
- **Verse**: 8–12 lines, 5–7 tokens per line, image-heavy.
- **Pre-chorus** (optional): 2–4 lines building tension.
- **Chorus**: 4–8 lines, heavy repetition, 4–6 tokens per line.
- **Bridge**: 4 lines, shift perspective or add a twist.
- **Outro**: repeat the hook or a stripped version.

### Hook placement rule
A hook line should appear at least 3 times in the song. The first appearance should be within the first 60 seconds of lyrics.

---

## 2. Line-Length Targets

| Section | Tokens per line | Purpose |
|---------|-----------------|---------|
| Verse | 5–7 | storytelling, imagery |
| Pre-chorus | 4–6 | tension, breathless build |
| Chorus | 3–5 | chant, singalong, dance floor |
| Bridge | 4–6 | contrast, emotional peak |

Avoid lines longer than 10 tokens unless used for comic/technological density (e.g., "Ja s mačetom igr'o često chess i četov'o").

---

## 3. Theme Palette

Use 1–2 primary themes and 1 secondary theme.

- **Love/obsession**: most common; pair with self-destruction, distance, or power imbalance.
- **Nightlife/club identity**: party as pride, party as escape, party as cultural statement.
- **Balkan pride**: regional references, language-as-identity, anti-melancholy optimism.
- **Modern technology/AI**: love through screens, generational labels, gaming/crypto metaphors.
- **Luxury/paranoia**: diamonds, gold, debt, insomnia, surveillance.

## 5. Rhyme & Sound Patterns

- **End-rhyme schemes**: AABB or ABAB in verses; chorus lines often rhyme in pairs.
- **Assonance**: long *a* and *o* sounds dominate ("zlatom / ledeno", "asfalt / pakla").
- **Internal rhyme**: use within chorus lines for momentum ("Bruji, bruji čaršija").
- **Alliteration**: stack words with same initial sound for a hook ("fobije, magije, utopije, nostalgije").
- **Phonetic substitution**: replace English sounds with Serbian equivalents ("yes" → "da", "kiss" → "mwah").

---

## 6. Hook Formulas

### Formula A: Action + Object
> [Verb] [my body / my heart / the night], [verb], [object]

Example: *"Savij moje telo, savij origami"*

### Formula B: Identity Chant
> [I am / We are] [X] / [Y] / [Z]

Example: *"Ja sam glas iz računara / Telefonska industrija"*

### Formula C: Bilingual Phonetic Taunt
> [English phrase] / [Serbian correction]

Example: *"Nije yes, yes, yes / Nego da, da, da"*

### Formula D: Negation Loop
> [No, no, no] / [Consequence]

Example: *"Nema, nema, nema me / Gazim, za mnom gori put"*

### Formula E: Rumor/Atmosphere Loop
> [Repetition of environment] / [Who I am]

Example: *"Bruji, bruji čaršija / To je ona moja muškarčina"*

---

## 7. Poetic Devices That Work

- **Anaphora**: repeating the same word at the start of successive lines.
- **Paradox**: "I choose you / I don't need you".
- **Hyperbole**: "even if the road leads to hell".
- **Oxymoron**: "gold chain / cold heart".
- **Metonymy**: using *čaršija* (market) for gossip, *rakija* for energy.
- **Code-switching**: drop English words for tech, fashion, or crowd commands.

---

## 8. English Injection Rules

- Keep English under 20% of total tokens for most tracks; 30% only for explicit bilingual gimmicks.
- Use English for: interjections, tech terms, brand/culture references, hook sounds.
- Avoid English in narrative verses unless the character is code-switching naturally.

---

## 9. Quality Checklist

- [ ] Hook appears at least 3 times.
- [ ] Chorus lines are under 6 tokens each.
- [ ] At least one concrete sensory noun per verse.
- [ ] One regional or cultural reference somewhere in the song.
- [ ] A contradiction or paradox in the hook or pre-chorus.
- [ ] Repetition feels intentional, not accidental.
- [ ] The last line of the chorus is the most memorable phrase.

## 5a. Prosody Rules
<!-- Source: R3 (researcher_pop_hook_science_20260806.md) -->

### Vowel Placement (7 rules, adapted for Serbian 5-vowel system)

1. **Place /a/ on the highest belted notes** — open, low vowel; easiest to belt, maximum vocal tract openness.
2. **Place /o/ on sustained high notes** — open-mid back vowel; second-easiest belting vowel.
3. **Avoid /i/ on sustained high notes** — close front vowel causes constriction; "gives singers fits."
4. **Avoid /u/ on high notes** — close back vowel; high tongue position mutes the vocal tract.
5. **No diphthongs on sustained notes** — Serbian has no true diphthongs, so this is a natural advantage over English.
6. **Leverage the five-vowel system** — /a/ and /o/ are belt-friendly; /e/ is mid (usable with care); /i/ and /u/ need modification on ascending pitches.
7. **Modify close vowels toward open configurations on ascending pitches** — "tune" /i/ toward /e/, /u/ toward /o/.

### Stress Matching (5 rules)

8. **Match stressed syllables with stressed beats** (downbeat and beat 3 in 4/4) — Pattison's "Perfect Match" principle.
9. **Don't place prepositions, articles, or conjunctions on strong beats** — they are "sign holders," not "workers."
10. **Place nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs on strong beats** — they "sweat" and deserve "sweaty notes."
11. **Avoid 4+ syllable words on downbeats** — they get clipped and sound rushed.
12. **Keep unstressed syllables on unstressed beats** (beats 2 and 4 in 4/4) — the 4/4 architectonic stress pattern must align with natural language stress.

### Phrasing (5 rules)

13. **Front-heavy phrases** (starting on downbeat) feel stable, anchored — use for confident, resolved statements.
14. **Back-heavy phrases** (starting after downbeat) feel unstable, in motion — use for longing, questioning, unresolved ideas.
15. **Keep syllable counts consistent across parallel verses** — same melody requires same syllable pattern; if verse 1 line 1 has 8 syllables, verse 2 line 1 must have 8.
16. **Break lines longer than 12 syllables into 2–8 syllable subparts** using melodic contrast, rhythmic contrast, or rests.
17. **Weak-bar phrasing** feels floaty, existential — use for emotional vulnerability or regret.

### Structural Prosody (3 rules)

18. **Stable lyrics need stable harmony** (resolved chords, perfect rhyme) — unstable lyrics need unstable harmony (unresolved chords, assonance rhyme).
19. **Verse lyrics should "answer" what the chorus asks** — the verse sets up what the chorus delivers.
20. **Save the highest note for the second half of a section** — maximizes emotional payoff (Max Martin principle).

## 6a. Hook Construction Toolkit

| # | Technique | Description | Serbian/Bosnian Application |
|---|-----------|-------------|-----------------------------|
| 1 | **Melodic Preview** | Plant a fragment of chorus melody in the verse so the chorus feels familiar on first hearing | Use the chorus melody for a verse line about the same topic; listeners feel they've heard the hook before it arrives |
| 2 | **Glue Hook** | Repeat the same key lyric in different sections (verse + chorus) to create cross-section familiarity | Echo the hook phrase ("Nema me") once in the verse before the chorus uses it as the main hook (extends Formula D: Negation Loop) |
| 3 | **FuelCore Hook** | Split hook into a memorable core (repeated title) and energizing fuel (denser surrounding lines) | Core: 2–3 syllable chant ("Da, da, da"); fuel: faster 8–10 syllable lines around it (extends Formula C: Bilingual Phonetic Taunt) |
| 4 | **Standout** | Small gestural interjections given foreground space and mix volume (HEY, WOO, ugh, duh) | Use "ej!", "hej!", "ho!" with enough space and mix volume to function as secondary hooks |
| 5 | **Syllable Math** | Equalize syllable counts across parallel lines using vowel elongation or consonant addition | If line 1 has 5 syllables and line 2 has 7, elongate a vowel ("da-a-a") to balance both to 7 |
| 6 | **Growing Climax** | Each chorus return is bigger than the last — add layers, juxtapose melodies, increase production | First chorus: vocal + beat. Second chorus: add ad-libs. Final chorus: layered vocals + instrumental counter-melody |
| 7 | **Section Contrast** | Sharp contrasts between adjacent sections (pitch, rhythm, lyrics, production) with 1–2 carried elements | Verse: low, fast, dense. Chorus: high, slow, sparse. Keep the same drum pattern for orientation |
| 8 | **Intro-as-Chorus** | Use the chorus melody (or its key fragment) as the intro, so the chorus feels pre-established | Open with the hook instrumental or a stripped vocal version before verse 1 begins (ABBA / Max Martin trick) |
| 9 | **Chant Hook** | Simple, repetitive, low-melodic-range phrase designed for crowd singalong | 3–4 syllable repeated phrase on a single pitch: "Savij, savij, savij" (extends Formula A: Action + Object) |
| 10 | **Call-and-Response** | A lead line answered by a group response, creating participatory structure | Lead: "Ko je tu?" / Response: "Mi smo!" — works in club and splav settings |
| 11 | **Vocal Chop Hook** | Sliced, pitched, and sequenced vocal fragments functioning as a melodic-rhythmic instrument | Record a Serbian phrase, chop and pitch it as an instrumental hook between chorus sections |
| 12 | **Riserchorus** | Blend EDM riser intensity (rising pitch, rhythmic build, absent kick) with pop chorus memorability | Build tension with rising vocal repetition ("sve, sve, SVE, SVE") then drop into the full chorus |

## 10. Max Martin Principles for Serbian Pop
<!-- Source: R3 (researcher_pop_hook_science_20260806.md) -->

Eight principles distilled from Max Martin's documented methodology, adapted for Serbian/Bosnian pop.

1. **Melodic previews** — Plant chorus melody fragments in verses. When the chorus hits, the listener feels they've already heard it. Overrepresented in Martin's biggest hits vs. lower-charting songs.
2. **Glue hooks** — Repeat the title or key lyric across verse and chorus. Creates cross-section familiarity that compounds on each listen.
3. **FuelCore structure** — Separate the hook into a memorable core (the part everyone remembers) and energizing fuel (denser surrounding lines that add tension). The core is 2–4 syllables; the fuel is 6–10.
4. **Syllable math** — Equalize syllable counts across parallel lines. If one line has 5 and the next has 7, elongate a vowel or add a melisma to balance them. The "math" must be equal.
5. **Growing climaxes** — Second chorus bigger than first; final chorus biggest. Add layered vocals, instrumental counter-melodies, or juxtapose a verse melody over the chorus.
6. **Two-second recognition** — The song must be identifiable within two seconds. The opening instrumental or vocal immediately establishes identity.
7. **Chorus within 50 seconds** — Most Martin hits reach the first chorus before the one-minute mark, derived from club DJ culture where hesitation drives people off the floor.
8. **Lagom (balance)** — If the verse is busy with many notes, the chorus should be sparse. If the chorus is punchy and staccato, the verse should be loose and ambient. Swedish for "just the right amount."

## 11. Serbian Vowel Advantage
<!-- Source: R3 (researcher_pop_hook_science_20260806.md) -->

Serbian's five-vowel system (/a/, /e/, /i/, /o/, /u/) is structurally advantageous for pop hook construction compared to English's eleven monophthongs plus diphthongs.

- **Five vowels, no diphthongs** — English diphthongs (/eɪ/, /aɪ/, /aʊ/, /oʊ/) create sustained-note problems that Serbian singers never face. Serbian has only vowel clusters (sequences of monophthongs), not true diphthongs.
- **No vowel reduction** — English reduces unstressed vowels to schwa; Serbian maintains full vowel quality in all positions. Serbian lyrics retain vowel clarity even at fast tempos — an advantage for hook intelligibility.
- **All vowels in all positions** — English has distributional restrictions (some vowels cannot occur word-finally); Serbian vowels appear freely in any position, giving lyricists maximum flexibility for end-rhyme vowel choices.
- **/a/ and /o/ on high notes** — The two most belt-friendly vowels are the most frequent in Serbian pop lyrics (appearing in top words: da, sam, na, kad, sve, ja). Consciously place /a/ and /o/ on the highest melodic notes.
- **Pitch accent as prosodic dimension** — Serbian's four melodic accents (falling, rising, long-falling, long-rising) add a prosodic dimension absent in English. Matching accent direction with melodic direction can enhance memorability — an unexplored advantage.

## 12. Verse Development (Pattison)
<!-- Source: R1 (researcher_universal_lyric_principles_20260806.md) -->

Pat Pattison's verse development principle: each verse must advance the idea, not rephrase it. Stagnant verses kill the chorus.

### The I-You-We Formula

Assign each verse a different point of view to create natural progression:

- **Verse 1: 1st person ("I")** — Establish the narrator's experience. "I was so lonely / and then I met you."
- **Verse 2: 2nd person ("You")** — Shift focus to the other person. "You were the one who walked away."
- **Verse 3: 3rd person ("We/They")** — Zoom out to the broader picture. "We were never meant to last."

### Advancement Rules

- **Each verse must advance the idea** — if verse 2 says the same thing as verse 1 in different words, the song goes nowhere. The chorus needs verses that develop to "dance" when the refrain attaches.
- **Stagnant verses kill the chorus** — when verses don't advance, the refrain loses power on each return because the listener has received no new context.
- **Use separate ideas in separate systems** — instead of I-you-we, you can assign each verse a different time, place, or emotional angle, as long as each verse adds new information.
- **Test: read verses 1 and 2 back-to-back** — if you can swap them without changing the song's meaning, one is redundant.
